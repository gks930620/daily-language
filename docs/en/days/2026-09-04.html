환경·보건 — 분해되지 않는 '영원한 화학물질'(PFAS) 오염과 정화 비용을 누가 무는가(논설)

For decades, the compounds known as PFAS were prized for their indifference to heat, water and grease, and manufacturers embedded them in everything from firefighting foam to food packaging. That same durability, however, has proved to be their most damaging property: the carbon-fluorine bonds that make the chemicals so useful also render them almost impossible to break down, so they linger in soil and bloodstreams for decades. Regulators, long content to set advisory limits that carried no legal force, are now scrambling to impose enforceable ceilings on how much of the residue may be present in drinking water. Utilities counter that the burden has been misplaced, since the cost of installing filtration systems capable of stripping out contaminants measured in parts per trillion will ultimately fall on ratepayers rather than on the firms that profited from the compounds. Whether that grievance translates into a wholesale reallocation of liability depends less on toxicology than on the litigation now working its way through the courts, where a handful of settlements have already hinted at the scale of what remediation might cost.
